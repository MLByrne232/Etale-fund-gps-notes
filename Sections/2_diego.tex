\subsection{Some classical algebraic geometry}
\label{subsec:2_1_classical_AG}
How much can we know about spaces from their functions? A simple example here is the space $\C^n$. Consider the set $R$ of polynomial functions $\C^n \to \C$, which may be identified as $R=\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Then for each point $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n) \in \C^n$, we may define the evaluation map $\ev_p : R \to \C$ given by $\ev_p(f) := f(p)$. Clearly this is a surjective morphism of $\C$-algebras, so we can think of this as a map quotienting out by the maximal ideal 
\[
	\mxid_p := \ker(\ev_p) = (x_1-p_1,\ldots,x_n-p_n).
\] 
In fact, all maximal ideals of $R$ are of the form $\mxid_q$ for some $q \in \C^n$, so this defines a bijection
\[
	\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=tiny]
		\C^n \ar[r, leftrightarrow, "\sim"] & \Spec_\mxid(R) \\
		p \rar[mapsto] & \mxid_p 
	\end{tikzcd}
\]
where $\Spec_\mxid(R)$ denotes the \bemph{maximal spectrum} of $R$, that is, the set of maximal ideals of $R$. For each subset $U \subseteq \C^n$ and ideal $J \ideal R$, we may then extend this construction by defining 
\[
	\I(U) := \bigcap_{p \in U} \mxid_p, \quad \text{and} \quad \V(J) := \{p \in \C^n \mid \forall f \in J, \, f(p) = 0 \}.
\]
These two maps end up to be inclusion reversing, and thus define an antitone Galois connection
% https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsMixbMCwwLCJcXGxlZnRcXHsgXFxzdWJzdGFja3tcXHRleHR7U3Vic2V0c31cXFxcXFx0ZXh0e29mIH1cXENebn0gXFxyaWdodFxcfSJdLFsyLDAsIlxcbGVmdFxceyAgXFxzdWJzdGFja3tcXHRleHR7SWRlYWxzfSBcXFxcIFxcdGV4dHtvZiB9Un0gXFxyaWdodFxcfSJdLFswLDEsIkkiLDAseyJjdXJ2ZSI6LTJ9XSxbMSwwLCJWIiwwLHsiY3VydmUiOi0yfV1d
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	\begin{array}{c} \left\{ \bettersubstack{\text{Subsets}\\\text{of }\C^n} \right\} \end{array} && \begin{array}{c} \left\{  \bettersubstack{\text{Ideals} \\ \text{of }R} \right\} \end{array}
	\arrow["\I", curve={height=-12pt}, from=1-1, to=1-3]
	\arrow["\V", curve={height=-12pt}, from=1-3, to=1-1]
\end{tikzcd}\]
which restrict to inclusion reversing bijections between the \emph{radical ideals} of $R$ (i.e. those such that if $x^n \in J$ then $x \in J$), and the so-called \emph{algebraic sets} (this is \emph{Hilbert's Nullstellensatz}).

\begin{defn}
	A subset $X \subseteq \C^n$ is called an \bemph{algebraic subset} if it is defined to be the common zero locus of a set of $n$-variable polynomials in $\C^n$.
\end{defn}

We note that:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\C^n = \V(0)$, so $\C^n$ is an algebraic subset of itself;
	\item $\varnothing = \V(1)$, so $\varnothing$ is an algebraic subset of itself;
	\item as a consequence of \emph{Hilbert's Basis Theorem} (i.e. that $R$ is Noetherian), arbitrary intersections of algebraic subsets are algebraic; and
	\item as a consequence of the fact $\V(f) \cup \V(g) = \V(fg)$, finite unions of algebraic subsets are algebraic.
\end{itemize}
All of these properties say that the algebraic subsets define the closed sets of a topology on $\C^n$, which we call the \bemph{Zariski topology} $\mathcal{T}_\text{Zar}$. That is, a subset of $\C^n$ is Zariski-closed if and only if it is algebraic. 

\begin{defn}
	When $\C^n$ (or equivalently, $k^n$ for a field $k$) is endowed with the Zariski topology $\mathcal{T}_\text{Zar}$, it is referred to as (complex) \bemph{affine $n$-space} $\A^n$. A Zariski closed subset of $\A^n$ endowed with the subspace topology is referred to as a (complex) \bemph{closed affine algebraic variety} (or just a \emph{CAAV}).
\end{defn}

We can check that a basis for the open sets of the Zariski topology is given by open sets of the form
\[
	D_f := \{p \in \A^n \mid f(p) \neq 0\},
\]
which are huge for nonzero polynomials $f\in R$ (cf. Figure \ref{fig:zariski_open})! Because of this, CAAVs are very poorly behaved topological spaces (e.g. they're not Hausdorff), so continuous maps should not necessarily be our notion of a `nice' map. We are usually more interested in polynomial functions on these spaces, which preserve this algebraic structure.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/zariski_open.png}
	\caption{The basic open set $D_{y-x^2}$ in $\A^2$ shaded in light green.}
	\label{fig:zariski_open}
\end{figure}

\begin{eg}
	Let $X = \V(x^2 + y^2 - 1) \subseteq \A^2$ be the circle in the plane\footnote{While we are usually considering things over $\C$, which has topological dimension 2, the algebraic dimension of affine $n$-space is $n$ and so $\A^1$ is referred to as \emph{the line}, $\A^2$ as \emph{the plane}, etc.}. What would the polynomial functions $X \to \A^1$ even be? Well, these should be the restriction of a polynomial function $\A^2 \to \A^1$ to $X$, considered equal if and only if they agree at all points of $X$. The polynomial functions $\A^2 \to \A^1$ are just 2-variable complex polynomials, $\C[x,y]$, and two polynomials $f, g \in \C[x,y]$ agree on $X$ if and only their difference $f-g$ is zero on $X$. The polynomials which are zero on $X$, by definition, are the elements of the vanishing ideal $\I(X) = (x^2 + y^2 - 1)$, and so the polynomial functions $X \to \A^1$ may be identified with elements of the quotient $\C[x, y]/(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$.
\end{eg}

\begin{defn}
	For a closed affine algebraic variety $X \subseteq \A^n$, its \bemph{coordinate ring} $\ints(X)$ is the ring of polynomial functions $X \to \A^1$, that is, it is the quotient ring 
	\[
		\ints(X) := \frac{\C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]}{\I(X)}.
	\]
\end{defn}

We note a few things about coordinate rings of CAAVs over $\C$:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the coordinate ring $\ints(X)$ is a $\C$-algebra, since constant functions are polynomial;
	\item since $\I(X)$ is radical and $\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is an ID, the quotient $\ints(X)$ is \emph{reduced} (it has no nilpotents);
	\item since $\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is Noetherian and finitely-generated as a $\C$-algebra, $\ints(X)$ is finitely-generated as a $\C$-algebra.
\end{itemize}

This association of a coordinate ring to each CAAV over $\C$ may be seen to give an equivalence of categories
\[
	\begin{tikzcd}
		\left\{\bettersubstack{\text{closed, affine,} \\ \text{algebraic varieties} \\ \text{over }\C}\right\} \rar[leftrightarrow, "\simeq"] & \left\{\bettersubstack{\text{finitely generated,}\\ \text{reduced, commutative} \\ \C\text{-algebras}}\right\}^{\text{op}}
	\end{tikzcd}
\]
The fully-faithful property is pretty easy to see (our morphisms of CAAVs are just polynomial maps), but the essential surjectivity on objects is a bit more tricky. To show this we think back to the observation that at the beginning of the chapter that points of $\A^n$ are in bijection with maximal ideals of $\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$.\\ 

It is not too hard to show that this also holds of any complex CAAV $X$: that points of $X$ are in bijection with maximal ideals of $\ints(X)$. So if we endow $\Spec_\mxid(\ints(X))$ with the Zariski topology, we can view the maximal spectrum \emph{itself} as a CAAV.\\ 

To do this we only need to recall that the evaluation map $\ev_p$ may be seen as the quotient by $\mxid_p$, so the `evaluation' of a `function' $f \in \ints(X)$ at a `point' $\mxid_1 \in \Spec_\mxid(\ints(X))$ is defined to be the residue class $f \mod{\mxid}$. Translating the definition of the basic open sets of $X$ into this language, a basis for the Zariski topology on $\Spec_\mxid (\ints(X))$ is given by the sets of the form 
\[
	D_f := \{ \mxid_1 \in \Spec_\mxid(\ints(X)) \mid f \notin \mxid_1 \}.
\]
Given a finitely generated, reduced, commutative $\C$-algebra $A$, its maximal spectrum $\Spec_\mxid(A)$ under the Zariski topology may then be seen as a closed affine algebraic variety over $\C$ whose coordinate ring is $A$, giving essential surjectivity. To repeat, 
\begin{center}
	a closed affine algebraic variety $X$ \textbf{is} the maximal\\spectrum of its coordinate ring $\ints(X)$:
	\[
		X = \Spec_\mxid(\ints(X))
	\]
\end{center}
As for why this is functorial, the morphism of CAAVs corresponding to an algebra homomorphism $\varphi : A \to B$ is 
\[
	\Spec_\mxid(\varphi) : \Spec_\mxid(B) \to \Spec_\mxid(A), \qquad \mxid_B \mapsto  \phi^{-1}(\mxid_B).
\]


\subsubsection{Why move to schemes?}
\label{subsubsec:2_1_1_why_schemes}
\begin{itemize}
	\item We want to create a similar theory that accepts \emph{any} commutative ring, rather than exclusively finitely generated, reduced, commutative $\C$-algebras;
	\item Since algebraic subsets correspond to radical ideals, this theory does not see points with multiplicity/rings with nilpotence. For example, the equation $x^2 =0$ in $\A^1$ should correspond to a point at zero \emph{with multiplicity 2}, but $\V(x^2) = \V(\sqrt{(x^2)}) = \V(x)$ which only sees that there is a point at zero and forgets the multiplicity;
	\item While $\Spec_\mxid$ is functorial for our FGRC $\C$-algebras, it fails to be functorial in general (preimages of maximal ideals under general ring homomorphisms are rarely maximal, e.g. $(0)$ in $\Z \to \Q$). 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Affine schemes}
\label{subsec:2_2_affine_schemes}
In much the same way that the theory of varieties revolves around the maximal spectrum, the theory of schemes is based on the \bemph{spectrum} of a commutative ring $R$. This is the set of \emph{prime} ideals of $R$, which we also endow with the Zariski topology 
\[
	\Spec(R) = \{\prid \ideal R \ \text{prime}\}, \qquad D_f = \{\prid \in \Spec(R) \mid f \notin \prid\}, \qquad \mathcal{T}_\text{Zar} = \gen{D_f \mid f \in R}.
\]

For simplicity, we will assume from now on that all rings $R$ are integral domains unless otherwise specified, but bear in mind that all of this can be done over non-integral domains (the definition of localisation does change in this case though).

\begin{defn}
	An \bemph{affine scheme} is a pair $(X, \ints_X)$, where $X = \Spec(R)$ for some commutative ring $R$, and $\ints_X$ is a sheaf\footnote{If you don't know what a sheaf is, I've included \hyperref[appendix:A_sheaves]{a short appendix} on everything you'll need to know about them.} on $X$ (called the \bemph{structure sheaf}) defined on the basic Zariski-open sets $D_f$ to be the localisation of $R$ at $f$
	\[
		\ints_X (D_f) := R_f = R\left[\frac{1}{f}\right],
	\]
	i.e. those elements of the field of fractions $\Frac(R)$ of the form $g / f^n$, where $g \in R$ and $n \in \Z$. The restriction morphisms $\res:\ints_X(D_f) \to \ints_X(D_{fg})$ is the usual inclusion map. The ring $\ints(X) := \ints_X(X)$ of global sections is called the \bemph{coordinate ring} of $X$.
\end{defn}

We can check that the stalks of $\ints_X$ are given by
\[
	\ints_{X, \prid} = \colim\limits_{U \ni \prid} \ints_X(U) = R_\prid,
\]
the localisation of $R$ at $\prid$, i.e. those elements of $\Frac(R)$ whose denominator is not in $\prid$. Since $R_{\prid}$ is local, affine schemes are locally ringed spaces. If a locally ringed space $(X, \ints)$ is isomorphic to an affine scheme, we will call it an affine scheme also.\\

As before, for each $\prid \in \Spec(R)$ we define the \bemph{evaluation map} $\ev_\prid$ to be the quotient map with kernel $\prid$, although now it is valued in the \bemph{residue field} $k(\prid) := \Frac(R/\prid) = R_\prid / \prid R_\prid$. This allows us to define our $\V$ and $\I$ functions as before:
\begin{align*}
	\V(J) &:= \{\prid \in \Spec(R) \mid \ev_\prid(f)=0, \ \forall f \in J\} = \{\prid \in \Spec(R) \mid J \subseteq \prid\},\\
	\I(U) &:= \{f \in R \mid \ev_\prid(f) = 0, \ \forall \prid \in U\} = \bigcap_{\prid \in U} \prid,
\end{align*}
where $J\ideal R$ is an ideal and $U \subseteq \Spec(R)$ is a subset. Notably, we see that:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the Zariski-closure $\ol{U}$ of a subset $U \subseteq \Spec(R)$ is exactly $\ol{U} = \V\I(U)$;
	\item the correspondence theorem ensures that $\V(J) \subseteq \Spec(R)$ may be identified with the affine scheme $\Spec(R/J)$, so the Zariski-closed subsets are the spectra of quotients of $R$ (and $\varnothing$);
	\item properties of localisation ensure that $D_f \subseteq \Spec(R)$ may be identified with the affine scheme $\Spec(R_f)$, so the basic Zariski-open subsets are the spectra of localisations of $R$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{eg}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item The affine scheme $\Spec(R[x_1,\ldots,x_n])$ is again called \bemph{affine $n$-space} $\A^n$, and to specify the ring $R$ we say that it is \bemph{defined over} $R$. This is sometimes denoted $\A^n_R$ or $\A^n_{/R}$.
		\item While we have seen that the closed affine algebraic variety $\Spec_\mxid(\C[x,y])$ may be identified with $\C^n$ under the Zariski topology, we instead look at the prime spectrum $\Spec(\C[x,y])$. As a set, this decomposes as 
			\[
				\Spec(\C[x,y]) = \Spec_\mxid (\C[x,y]) \,\, \cup \,\, \{(0)\} \,\, \cup \,\, \{(f) \mid f \in \C[x,y] \text{ irr.}\}.
			\]
			These different categories of points are distinguished by the Zariski closures of their corresponding singletons, which is exactly $\V\I(\{\prid\})=\V(\prid)$.
			\begin{itemize}
				\item For a point $\mxid_1 \in \Spec_\mxid(\C[x,y])$, i.e. a maximal ideal of $\C[x,y]$, we see that $\ol{\{\mxid_1\}} = \V(\mxid_1)$ is the set of prime ideals containing $\mxid_1$, which is exactly the singleton $\{\mxid_1\}$ since $\mxid_1$ is maximal. For this reason, maximal ideals of $\C[x,y]$ correspond to \bemph{closed points} of $\Spec(\C[x,y])$ (and this works for general rings!);
				\item The point $(0) \in \Spec(\C[x,y])$, i.e. the zero ideal, has $\ol{\{(0)\}} = \V(0) = \Spec(\C[x,y])$. For this reason, the point $(0)$ is called a \bemph{dense point} or a \bemph{generic point} of $\Spec(\C[x,y])$ (and again, this works for general rings);
				\item A point $(f) \in \Spec(\C[x,y])$ with $f \in \C[x,y]$ irreducible has $\ol{\{(f)\}} = \V(f) = \Spec(R/(f))$, which is a closed affine subscheme (i.e. a Zariski closed subset). Such points are thus the generic points of closed affine subschemes of $\Spec(\C[x,y])$.
			\end{itemize}
			So the points of $\Spec(\C[x,y])$ may be thought of as
			\[
				\Spec(\C[x,y]) = \left\{\bettersubstack{\text{closed} \\ \text{points}}\right\} \,\, \cup \,\, \left\{\bettersubstack{\text{generic} \\ \text{point}}\right\} \,\, \cup \,\, \left\{\bettersubstack{\text{generic points of} \\ \text{closed subschemes}}\right\}
			\]
			We may think of generic points as a sort of `codimension 0 point', which is close to everything. 
		\item The affine scheme $\Spec(\C[x]/(x^2))$ decomposes as
			\[
				\Spec\left(\frac{\C[x]}{(x^2)}\right) = \{(x)\} \,\,\cup\,\, \{(0)\}
			\]
			which is just the one closed point, along with the generic point. This is seen to be different to $\Spec(\C[x]/(x)) = \Spec(\C)$, which consists solely of a generic point. So our notion is tracking multiplicity correctly!
		\item One interesting example that may motivate something later: if we consider the prime spectrum of $\Z$, this may be visualised as a long line (corresponding to the generic point) connecting infinitely many points (corresponding to the closed points, the ideals $p\Z$), as in Figure \ref{fig:SpecZ}.
		\begin{figure}[H]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Images/Spec(Z).png}
			\caption{$\Spec(\Z)$, per the \href{https://www.lmfdb.org/NumberField/1.1.1.1}{LMFDB}.}
			\label{fig:SpecZ}
		\end{figure}
		The inclusion $\Z \mono \Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$ gives us a corresponding surjective morphism $\Spec(\!\Z[\sqrt{3}]) \epi \Spec(\Z)$ which may be visualised as in Figure \ref{fig:SpecZsqrt3_over_SpecZ}, where the vertical columns are the fibres of the morphism.
		\begin{figure}[H]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Images/ramification_diagram_Zsqrt3.png}
			\caption{$\Spec(\Z[\!\sqrt{3}])\epi\Spec(\Z)$, per the \href{https://www.lmfdb.org/NumberField/2.2.12.1}{LMFDB}.}
			\label{fig:SpecZsqrt3_over_SpecZ}
		\end{figure}
		The behaviour of different points fall into three classes, all depicted differently in this diagram:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \textbf{A single, black point} above a point $(p)$ of $\Spec(\Z)$ corresponds to the ideal $p\Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$ being a prime ideal of $\Z[\sqrt{3}]$, as we see with e.g. $(17)$. This point $17\Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$ of $\Spec(\Z[\!\sqrt{3}])$ is a \bemph{reduced point}, i.e. a point with multiplicity 1. In the number theory lingo, we say that $17$ is \bemph{inert} in the extension $\Q(\!\sqrt{3})/\Q$;
			\item \textbf{Two different black points} above a point $(p)$ of $\Spec(\Z)$ corresponds to the ideal $p\Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$ decomposing as a product of two distinct prime ideals $\Prid_1, \Prid_2$ in $\Z[\sqrt{3}]$ each with multiplicity 1, as we see with e.g. $(11)$ decomposing in $\Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$ as $(1-2\sqrt{3})(1+2\sqrt{3})$. Both of these points $\Prid_1, \Prid_2$ above $(p)$ are again reduced points of $\Spec(\Z[\!\sqrt{3}])$. In the number theory lingo, we say that $11$ is \bemph{completely split} in $\Q(\!\sqrt{3})/\Q$, and we say that $(1-2\sqrt{3})$ and $(1+2\sqrt{3})$ are the primes \bemph{above} $11$;
			\item \textbf{A single, yellow point} above a point $(p)$ of $\Spec(\Z)$ corresponds to the ideal $p\Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$ being the square\footnote{...or more generally, a power of a single prime ideal.} of a single prime ideal $\Prid$ in $\Z[\!\sqrt{3}]$, as we see with e.g. $3\Z[\!\sqrt{3}] = (\sqrt{3})^2$. This point $(\sqrt{3})$ is a \bemph{non-reduced point} of multiplicity 2. In the number theory lingo, we see that $3$ is \bemph{totally ramified} in $\Q(\!\sqrt{3})/\Q$, and we say that $(\sqrt{3})$ is the prime above $3$.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

By construction, we get a similar equivalence of categories
\[
	\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=tiny]
		\mathbf{CRing} \rar[leftrightarrow, "\simeq"] & \mathbf{AffSch}^\text{op}\\
		R \rar[mapsto] & (\Spec(R), \ints_{\Spec(R)})
	\end{tikzcd}
\]
between the categories of commutative rings and the (opposite) category of affine schemes. By definition of affine schemes, this map is clearly essentially surjective. The more difficult thing to prove here is that all morphisms of affine schemes are induced by ring homomorphisms. We omit this proof.




\subsubsection{A quick note on abstract schemes}
\label{subsubsec:2_2_1_general_schemes}
While we have defined the notion of an affine scheme, there is a more general notion of a scheme which is a strictly larger category. Very roughly, affine schemes are to schemes what $\C^n$ is the a complex manifold: a scheme should be a space that locally looks like an affine scheme, or one that results from gluing together affine schemes.

\begin{defn}
	A locally ringed space $(X, \ints)$ is called a \bemph{scheme} if there is an open cover $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $X$ such that each $U_i$, as a locally ringed space, is isomorphic to an affine scheme $\Spec(R_i)$. A \bemph{morphism of schemes} is then a map of locally ringed spaces between schemes.
\end{defn}

\begin{rmk}
	A quick etymological note: the reason why we use the somewhat-ominous word \emph{scheme} for these things is due to a slight mistranslation. Originally called \emph{sch\'emas} in French by Chevalley, this should have been translated to \emph{schema}, i.e. a universally applicable outline of a general concept (per Wiktionary). Indeed, the word \emph{schema} is much more apt, since they allow us to define sets of points via a general rule for every $R$-algebra, as we shall see.
\end{rmk}

\begin{eg}
	\label{eg:abstract_schemes_and_Proj}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item Let $X = \A^2$ be affine $2$-space, say over $\C$. Then $U := \A^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ defines a Zariski-open subset, which is covered by the Zariski opens 
			\[
				U_1 = \Spec\left(\frac{\C[x,y,z]}{(xz-1)}\right) \qquad \text{and} \qquad U_2 = \Spec\left(\frac{\C[x,y,z]}{(yz-1)}\right).
			\]
			Since $U$ has an open cover by affine schemes, it is itself a scheme. However, since every regular function on $U$ (i.e. locally rational) extends uniquely to a regular function on $X$, the map $\ints(X) \to \ints(U)$ induced by the inclusion $U \mono X$ is an isomorphism. But $U \mono X$ is not itself an isomorphism, meaning that $U$ cannot be affine by the equivalence of categories (only isomorphisms should induce isomorphisms).
		\item \textbf{Proj construction:} If $R = \bigoplus_{i \geq 0} R_i$ is a commutative graded ring, we define the ideal $R_+ = \bigoplus_{i>0} R_i$ of positive-degree elements. An ideal $J \ideal R$ is \bemph{homogeneous} if it is generated by homogeneous elements (not necessarily of the same degree). Let $Z := \Proj(R)$ denote the set of prime homogeneous ideals of $R$ which do not contain $R_+$. Then we define the \bemph{projective Zariski topology} $\mathcal{T}_\text{Zar}^\PP$ to be given by the basic opens $D_0(f) = \{\prid \in Z \mid f \notin \prid\}$, where $f \in R_d$ is a homogeneous element of $R$.\\ 

		We then see that the open set $D_0(f)$ may be identified with $\Proj(R_f)$, which may further be identified with $\Spec(R_{f,0})$ as a topological space, where $R_{f,0}$ is the subring of degree-zero homogeneous elements of $R_f$. We may then define the structure sheaf $\ints_Z$ on $Z$ by identifying $D_0(f)=\Spec(R_{f,0})$ as \emph{affine schemes}, so $\ints_Z(D_0(f)) = R_{f,0}$. By construction, we see that $\Proj(R)$ is a scheme, but it is not affine.
		\item[(iiia)] As a specific example of the Proj construction, we define \bemph{projective $n$-space} $\PP^n$ over $R$ to be the scheme
			\[
				\PP^n := \Proj(R[x_0,\ldots, x_n]),
			\]
			where $R[x_0,\ldots, x_n]$ is given its canonical grading by degree. When $R = \R$ or $R= \C$, this agrees with the classical notion of $\R\PP^n$ or $\C\PP^n$, insomuchas the closed points naturally identify with these manifolds when endowed with the Euclidean topology. As with affine $n$-space, this is sometimes denoted $\PP^n_R$ or $\PP^n_{/R}$.
		\item[(iiib)] Alternatively, we can define $\PP^n$ via gluing: in the case $n=1$, we may two copies of $R[y]$ as subrings of $R[t, t^{-1}]$, namely $R[t]$ and $R[t^{-1}]$. Letting $X = \Spec(R[u]), Y=\Spec(R[v])$ be two copies of the affine line, we then get two maps
			\[
				\varphi_*: \Spec(R[t,t^{-1}]) \to X,\qquad \psi_*:\Spec(R[t,t^{-1}]) \to Y,
			\]
			induced by these two maps $\varphi: R[u] \to R[t,t^{-1}]; u \mapsto t$ and $\psi: R[v] \to R[t,t^{-1}]; v \mapsto t^{-1}$, and we can naturally identify their images as schemes. Gluing along this identification, we may define
			\[
				\PP^1 := \frac{X \sqcup Y}{\varphi_*(x) \sim \psi_*(x)}
			\]
			and then since structure sheaves agree on $\im(\varphi_*)$ and $\im(\psi_*)$, the structure sheaf of $\PP^1$ is well-defined by picking affine charts. We similarly define $\PP^n$ by defining polynomial rings 
			\[
				A_i = R[u_{i,0}, \ldots, \wh{u_{i,i}}, \ldots, u_{i,n}],\qquad B_{i,j} = R[t_{i,1}, \ldots, \wh{t_{i,i}}, \ldots, t_{i,n}, t_{i,j}^{-1}],
			\]
			for each $j \neq i$, where the hat denotes that $u_{i,i}$ and $t_{i,i}$ are omitted. We then get affine charts $X_i := \Spec(A_i) \cong \A^n$ with open immersions $\Spec(B_{i,j}) \to X_i$ and $\Spec(B_{i,j}) \to X_j$ induced by the maps 
			\begin{align*}
				\varphi_i : 	A_i &\to B_{i,j} & \psi_i : A_j &\to B_{i,j}\\
								u_{i,k} &\mapsto t_{i,k} & u_{j,i} &\mapsto t_{i,j}^{-1}\\
										&			 & u_{j,k} &\mapsto t_{i,k} t_{i,j}^{-1}\,\,\,(k\neq i,j)
			\end{align*}
			We then define 
			\[
				\PP^n = \frac{\coprod_{i=0}^n \Spec(A_i)}{\varphi_{i,*}(x) \sim \psi_{i, *}(x)}
			\]
			Once again, this also defines the structure sheaf on $\PP^n$. \qedhere
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

One very important notion is that of a scheme being \emph{above} another scheme.

\begin{defn}
	\label{defn:S-scheme}
	If $S$ is a scheme, then an \bemph{$S$-scheme} is a scheme $X$ equipped with a morphism $\pi : X \to S$. If $R$ is a ring, then we may call a $\Spec(R)$-scheme simply an \bemph{$R$-scheme}. A \bemph{morphism} $f:(X_1, \pi_1) \to (X_2, \pi_2)$ of $S$-schemes is a morphism of schemes that commutes with the maps $\pi_i: X_i \to S$, i.e. such that $\pi_2 \circ f = \pi_1$.
\end{defn}

\begin{eg}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item Any affine scheme $\Spec(R)$ is a $\Z$-scheme in a unique way, since any commutative ring admits a unique homomorphism $\Z \to R$;
		\item Affine $n$-space $\A^n$ over $R$ is an $R$-scheme because $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is naturally an $R$-algebra;
		\item Any extension $L/K$ of fields makes $\Spec(L)$ a $K$-scheme;
		\item Any extension $L/K$ of number fields makes $\Spec(\ints_L)$ an $\ints_K$-scheme, and if $S$ is a set of primes of $\ints_K$ then $\Spec(\ints_{L,S})$ becomes an $\ints_{K,S}$-scheme (where $\ints_{K,S}$ denotes the $S$-integers of $K$ and similarly for $L$);
		\item The abstract scheme $U:=\A^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ from Example \ref{eg:abstract_schemes_and_Proj}(i) has a canonical projection map to the affine scheme $S := \A^1 \setminus \{0\} = \Spec(\C[x,y]/(xy-1))$, making $U$ an $S$-scheme.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

\subsection{Fibre products and base change}
\label{subsec:2_3_operations_with_schemes}
Along with the notion of schemes and morphisms, we have a few notable operations we can do on schemes. The first and foremost is the notion of \bemph{fibre products}.

\begin{defn}
	If $\varphi : X\to Z$ and $\psi : Y \to Z$ are two morphisms of schemes, the \bemph{fibre product} $X \times_Z Y$ is the limit of the diagram $X \to Z \ot Y$, that is, it is universal with respect to the commutative diagram 
	\[
		\begin{tikzcd}
			X \times_Z Y \rar[dashed] \dar[dashed] & Y \dar["\psi"] \\
			X \rar["\varphi"] & Z
		\end{tikzcd}
	\]
	An important special case of the fibre product is the \bemph{base change} $X_A := X \times_{\Spec(R)} \Spec(A)$, where $X$ is an $R$-scheme and $A$ is a commutative $R$-algebra.
\end{defn}


\begin{eg}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item If $X:=\A^n_{R}$ is affine $n$-space over a commutative ring $R$ and $A$ is a commutative $R$-algebra, then the base change $X_A$ is exactly affine $n$-space over $A$;
		\item Similarly, if $X := \PP^n_{R}$ is projective $n$-space over a commutative ring $R$ and $A$ is a commutative $R$-algebra, then the base change $X_A$ is exactly projective $n$-space over $A$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

\begin{prop}
	\label{prop:fibre_product_affine_schemes}
	If $X = \Spec(A), Y = \Spec(B), Z=\Spec(R)$ are affine schemes with morphism $\varphi : X \to Z$ and $\psi : Y \to Z$ (making $A$ and $B$ commutative $R$-algebras), then their fibre product is $X \times_Z Y = \Spec(A \otimes_R B)$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
	By the equivalence of categories $\CRing^\text{op} \simeq \mathbf{AffSch}$, the pullback in $\mathbf{AffSch}$ corresponds to a pushout in $\CRing$, which are given exactly by tensor products.
\end{proof}

\begin{eg}
	Consider affine $n$- and $m$-space $\A^n, \A^m$ over $\C$. Let $C = \Spec(\C)$, which we may view as a point onto which both $\A^n$ and $\A^m$ project. The fibre product $\A^n \times_C \A^m$ may then be compute using Proposition \ref{prop:fibre_product_affine_schemes}: 
	\[
		\A^1 \times_C \A^1 = \Spec(\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n] \otimes_\C \C[y_1, \ldots, y_m]) = \Spec(\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n, y_1, \ldots, y_m]) = \A^{n+m}. \qedhere
	\]
\end{eg}

\subsection{Some special families of morphisms}
\label{subsec:2_4_families_of_morphisms}
Recall that we defined a morphism from a scheme $X$ to another scheme $Y$ to be a morphism $f:(X, \ints_X) \to (Y, \ints_Y)$ of the underlying locally ringed spaces. What does this actually mean for these functions? Well, continuity with respect to the Zariski topology should mean that the preimage of a subscheme is a subscheme, so a continuous function $f:X \to Y$ should be one that respects the "subscheme" heirarchy. The existence of the corresponding sheaf morphism $f^{\#} : \ints_Y \to f_*\ints_X$ just tells us that this also respects the underlying \emph{algebraic} structure.\\ 

In the affine case, the equivalence of categories $\CRing^{\text{op}} \simeq \mathbf{AffSch}$ tells us that it is enough to respect the algebraic structure (i.e. the global component $f^{\#}_Y : \ints(Y) \to \ints(X)$ of $f^{\#}$ fully determines the map $f$). More generally, morphisms of schemes restrict to morphisms of affine charts, so we can think of a morphism of a general scheme (in a very imprecise way) to be a bunch of ring homormophisms glued together.\\

As such, the usual families of ring homomorphisms (finite type, flat, etc.) may be extended to families of \emph{scheme} morphisms. In this section, we'll define all of the families of morphisms we need. 

\subsubsection{Finiteness criteria}
\label{subsubsec:2_4_1_finiteness}
First on the chopping block are the so-called finite-type morphisms. Recall that a homomorphism $f : A \to B$ of commutative rings is said to be of finite type if it realises $B$ as an $A$-algebra of finite-type (i.e. it is isomorphic to a quotient of some $A[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$).

\begin{defn}
	A morphism $f : \Spec(S) \to \Spec(R)$ of affine schemes is said to be of \bemph{finite type} if the associated ring homomorphism $f^{\#} : R \to S$ is finite-type. We say that a morphism $f : X \to Y$ of abstract schemes is \bemph{locally of finite type} if for every $x \in X$, there is an affine chart $U \ni x$ of $X$ and an affine chart $V \supseteq f[U]$ of $Y$ such that the restriction $f|_{U,V} : U \to V$ is of finite type.
\end{defn}

\begin{eg}
	\label{eg:finite_type_morphism}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item Let $n \leq m$ and consider affine $n$- and $m$-space $\A^n, \A^m$ over $\C$. The map 
		\[
			f:\A^m \epi \A^n; (x_0, \ldots, x_m) \mapsto (x_0, \ldots, x_n)
		\] 
		induced by inclusion $f^{\#} : \ints(\A^n) \mono \ints(\A^m)$ is of finite type, since the associated homomorphism $f^{\#}$ trivially realises $\C[x_1, \ldots, x_m]$ as a $\C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$-module;
		\item Now consider the affine scheme $\A^1\setminus\{0\} = \Spec(\C[x,x^{-1}])$ over $\C$. The map $w : \A^1\setminus\{0\} \epi \A^1 \setminus \{0\}; \, z \mapsto z^n$ with $w^{\#} : \C[x,x^{-1}] \mono \C[y, y^{-1}];\, x \mapsto y^n$, clearly realising $\C[y,y^{-1}]$ as a quotient of $\C[x,x^{-1}][u,v]$, namely
		\[
			\C[y,y^{-1}] \cong \frac{\C[x,x^{-1}][u,v]}{(u^n-x, v^n-x^{-1}, uv-1)},
		\]
		clearly by $y \mapsto u, y^{-1} \mapsto v = u^{-1}$;
		\item If $L/K$ is a finite extension of number fields, then inclusion $K \mono L$ induces the finite type morphism $\Spec(L) \epi \Spec(K)$, and similarly we get finite type morphisms $\Spec(\ints_L) \epi \Spec(\ints_K)$, and similarly for $S$-integers.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

There is a slightly stronger notion than finite-type called \emph{finite-presentation}. A homomorphism $f:A \to B$ of rings is of \bemph{finite presentation} if it makes $B$ a finitely \emph{finitely presented} $A$-algebra, i.e. it is a quotient of some $A[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ by some finitely generated ideal $(f_1, \ldots, f_m)$. 

\begin{defn}
	A morphism $f : \Spec(S) \to \Spec(R)$ of affine schemes is said to be of \bemph{finite presentation} if the associated ring homomorphism $f^{\#} : R \to S$ is finite-presentation. We say that a morphism $f : X \to Y$ of abstract schemes is \bemph{locally of finite presentation} if for every $x \in X$, there is an affine chart $U \ni x$ of $X$ and an affine chart $V \supseteq f[U]$ of $Y$ such that the restriction $f|_{U,V} : U \to V$ is of finite presentation.
\end{defn}

Note that if $A$ is Noetherian then so too is $A[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, so a morphism $f:A \to B$ is of finite presentation if and only if it is of finite type. Consequently, any (locally) finite-type morphism $X \to \Spec(A)$ is also (locally) of finite presentation.

\pagebreak

\begin{eg}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item All of the examples from Example \ref{eg:finite_type_morphism} are of finite presentation, since all fields and Dedekind domains are Noetherian;
		\item \textbf{Non-example}: if $k$ is a field, then the polynomial ring $A := k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$ in countably many variables is not Noetherian. The evaluation map $A \to k[x]$ sending $x_1 \mapsto x$ and $x_n  \mapsto 0$ for $n>1$ realises $k[x]$ as a finite-type $A$-algebra (in fact, with zero generators!), but the kernel $(x_2, x_3, x_4, \ldots)$ is not finitely generated over $A$. Thus the map $\A^1_k \to \Spec(A)$ induced by this is of finite type but not finite presentation.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

Most of the rings we work over will be Noetherian, so the distinction between morphisms of locally finite-type and of locally finite-presentation will be one that we will not pay much attention to.

\subsubsection{Relative differentials}
\label{subsubsec:2_4_2_relative_differentials}
To define the other properties we need, we will first have to talk about \emph{relative differentials} of ring homomorphisms. The idea comes from differential geometry: the differential forms on a manifold tell us a lot about that manifold's geometry. However, in algebraic geometry we have no analytic structure with which to define differential forms, but we can define derivations. The relative differentials then form some universal derivation, and thus encapsulate the idea of a differential of a function.

\begin{defn}
	Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $S$ a commutative $R$-algebra. Then an \bemph{$R$-derivation} on $S$ is an $R$-linear map $d : S \to M$ to an $S$-module $M$ satisfying the \bemph{Leibniz rule}:
	\[
		d(fg) = f\cdot dg + g\cdot d\!f.
	\]
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}
	Let $\varphi:R \to S$ be a homomorphism of commutative rings, making $S$ an $R$-algebra. Let $\wt{\Omega}$ denote the free $S$-module generated by the symbols $\{ds \mid s \in S\}$, and $N \leq \wt\Omega$ the submodule given by 
	\[
		N := \gen{d(\varphi r),\,\, d(s+t) - ds -dt,\,\, d(st) - s dt - t ds \mid r \in R, s,t \in S}.
	\]
	Then the quotient module $\Omega^1_{S\mid R} := \wt\Omega / N$ admits a canonical $R$-derivation $d : S \to \Omega^1_{S\mid R}$ sending each $s \in S$ to $ds \in \Omega^1_{S\mid R}$. Such an $R$-derivation $d: S \to \Omega^1_{S \mid R}$ is \emph{universal}, in that every other $R$-derivation $\delta : S \to M$ factors in the the form $\delta = \psi \circ d$, for $\psi : \Omega^1_{S \mid R} \to M$ a homomorphism of $S$-modules. Morover, if $\{s_1, \ldots, s_m\}$ generate $S$ as an $R$-algebra, then $\Omega^1_{S \mid R}$ is generated by $\{ds_1, \ldots, ds_m\}$ as an $S$-module.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
	\Exc. Prove this! (it's not too hard, most of the work is in the construction itself).
\end{proof}

\begin{defn}
	The module $\Omega^1_{S \mid R}$, equipped with its universal $R$-derivation $d : S \to \Omega^1_{S \mid R}$, is called the module of \bemph{relative differentials}, or \bemph{K\"ahler differentials}, of $S$ over $R$.
\end{defn}

\begin{eg}
	\label{eg:kahler_differentials_rings}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item Let $A = \C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, considered a $\C$-algebra. Then since $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ generate $A$ as a $\C$-algebra we can conclude, from $\C$-linearity of $\C$-derivations as well as the Leibniz rule, that $\Omega^1_{A \, \mid \, \C}$ is generated by the differentials $dx_1, \ldots, dx_n$. Thus, every K\"ahler differential $\omega \in \Omega^1_{A\, \mid\, \C}$ is of the form 
		\[
			\omega = f_1 dx_1 + \cdots + f_n dx_n, \qquad f_i \in A. 
		\]
		\item Suppose that $L/K$ is a finite separable extension of fields. Then by the primitive element theorem, $L = K(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha \in L$. Thus, $\Omega^1_{L \mid K}$ is generated by $d \alpha$. Let $m = X^n + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_i x^i \in K[x]$ denote the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $K$, which is notably a separable irreducible polynomial so shares no roots with its formal derivative $f'$. Then 
		\[
			m(\alpha) = \alpha^n + a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1 \alpha + a_0 = 0.
		\]
		Applying the derivation $d$ to both sides gives 
		\[
			0 = (n\alpha^{n-1} + (n-1)a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-2} + \cdots + 2 a_2\alpha + a_1)d\alpha = f'(\alpha)d\alpha.
		\]
		But $f'(\alpha) \neq 0$ since $f$ is separable irreducible, and thus we conclude that $d\alpha = 0$, hence $\Omega^1_{L \mid K}=0$. \qedhere
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

\begin{prop}
	\label{prop:differential_of_poly_is_derivative}
	Let $S$ be a commutative $R$-algebra (for $R$ a commutative ring) and consider the universal derivation $d : S \to \Omega^1_{S \mid R}$. If $p \in R[t]$ is a polynomial over $R$ and $\alpha \in S$, then $d(p(\alpha)) = p'(\alpha)d\alpha$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
	We may check that the usual power law $d(\alpha^n) = n\alpha^{n-1} d\alpha$ works by repeatedly using the Leibniz rule. WLOG we may write $p = \sum_{i=0}^n a_it^i$, then we compute that 
	\begin{align*}
		d(p(\alpha)) &= d\left(a_n \alpha^n + a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots a_1 \alpha + a_0\right)\\ 
		&= a_n d(\alpha^{n}) + a_{n-1} d(\alpha^{n-1}) + \cdots + a_1 d\alpha + d(a_0)\\ 
		&= n a_n \alpha^{n-1} d\alpha + (n-1)a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-2} d\alpha + \cdots + 2a_2\alpha d\alpha + a_1d\alpha\\ 
		&= p'(\alpha)d\alpha. \qedhere
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}

We can then extend this notion for schemes using our notion of $\ints_X$-modules (see \S\ref{subsubsec:A_4_1_O-modules}).

\begin{defn}
	If $f : Y \to X$ is a morphism of affine schemes, then the \bemph{sheaf of relative differentials} $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X}$ is the $\ints_X$-module on $X$ whose sections are given by $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X}(U) := \Omega^1_{f_*\ints_Y(U) \mid \ints_X(U)}$, with respect to the map $f^\#_U : \ints_X(U)\to f_*\ints_Y(U)$. For a morphism of general schemes $f: Y \to X$, we may define the sheaf $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X}$ by declaring it to be given by the above on the affine charts of $X, Y$.
\end{defn}

\begin{eg}
	\label{eg:kahler_differentials_schemes}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item Consider the $\C$-algebra homomorphim $\pi_n : \C[x,x^{-1}] \mono \C[t,t^{-1}]$ given by $x \mapsto t^n$. This corresponds to the morphism $\Pi_n$ of affine schemes given by 
			\[
				\Pi_n : \Gm' \epi \Gm; \quad z \mapsto z^n,
			\]
		where $\Gm = \Gm':= \Spec(\C[x,x^{-1}]) = \A^1_\C \setminus \{0\}$  are two copies of what is often called the \bemph{multiplicative group scheme} (we will see what this means later). We will then compute the sections $\Omega^1_{\Gm' \mid \Gm}$ on the basic open set $D_f \subseteq \Gm$ for some $f \in \C[x,x^{-1}]$. By definition $\Omega^1_{\Gm' \mid \Gm}(D_f)$ is the module of relative differentials of 
		\[
			B:=\Pi_{n, *}\ints_Y(D_f) = \C[t,t^{-1}]_{\pi_n (f)} \quad \text{over} \quad A:=\ints_X(D_f) = \C[x,x^{-1}]_f.
		\]
		The monomorphism $\pi_n$ identifies $A = \C[t^n, t^{-n}]_{\pi_n(f)} \subseteq B$, and thus we may view $B = A[x^{1 / n}, x^{-1 / n}]$. Writing $s_1 = x^{1/n}$ and $s_2 = x^{-1/n}$, we thus see that $\Omega^1_{\Gm'\mid\Gm}(D_f)$ is generated as a $B$-module by $\{ds_1, ds_2\}$. Applying the Leibniz rule to the equation $s_1 s_2 = 1$, we recover that 
		\[
			0 = s_1 ds_2 + s_2 ds_1,
		\]
		and consequently $ds_2 = -s_2^2 ds_1$ via $s_1^{-1} = s_2$. So $\Omega^1_{\Gm' \mid \Gm}(D_f)$ is generated only by $ds_1$. But the relation $s_1^n = x \in A$ tells us that $ns_1^{n-1} ds_1 = 0$, and since $ns_1^{n-1} \neq 0$ is invertible in $B$, we must have $ds_1 = 0$, hence $\Omega^1_{\Gm' \mid \Gm}(D_f)=0$. It follows from this that $\Omega^1_{\Gm' \mid \Gm}$ is zero on every open of $\Gm$, in such a case we write $\Omega^1_{\Gm' \mid \Gm}=0$;
		\item Recall the affine scheme $X:=\A^2_{\C} \setminus \{0\}$ with affine charts $U_1 := \Spec(\C[x, y, x^{-1}])$ and $U_2 := \Spec(\C[x, y, y^{-1}])$. The inclusion of $\C$ into the coordinate ring gives us a canonical projection onto $C := \Spec(\C)$. Relative differentials $\Omega^1_{X \mid C}(U_1)$ are generated by $\{dx, dy\}$ over $\C[x,y,x^{-1}]$ (we see $d(x^{-1}) = -x^{-2} dx$), and relative differentials $\Omega^1_{X \mid C}(U_2)$ are again generated by $\{dx, dy\}$, though this time over $\C[x,y,y^{-1}]$. One then computes that $\Omega^1_{X \mid C}(X) = \Omega^1_{X \mid C}(U_1 \cup U_2)$ is generated by $\{dx, dy\}$ over $\C[x,y]$. Heuristically, we may see $\Omega^1_{X \mid C}(X)$ as the set of differentials on $\Omega^1_{X \mid C}(U_1)$ which are defined on $U_2$, or vice versa;
		\item Suppose that $L/K$ is an extension of number fields, that it is unramified away from a finite set $S$ of prime ideals of $\ints_K$, and let $Y := \Spec(\ints_L)$ and $X := \Spec(\ints_K)$. Consider then the inclusion map $\pi^{\#} :\ints_K \mono \ints_L$, which induces an epimorphism $\pi : Y \epi X$ of affine schemes. We then wish to compute $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X}$ on, say, the basic open $D_f \subseteq X$. Computing these relative differentials then comes down to computing the relative differentials of the map $\pi^{\#} : \ints_{K, f} \mono \ints_{L, f}$. Since $L$ is a finite dimension $K$-vector space, it follows that $\ints_L$ is a finitely generated $\ints_K$-module, and so suppose we have some generators $\ints_L = \ints_K[\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n]$, and consequently $\ints_{L, f} = \ints_{K, f}[\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n]$. We know then that $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X}(D_f) = \Omega^1_{\ints_{L, f} \mid \ints_{K, f}}$ must be generated by $\{d\alpha_1, \ldots, d\alpha_n\}$ as an $\ints_{L, f}$-module. Since each $\alpha_i$ is algebraic over $K$, and in particular lies in the integral closure of $\ints_K$, we may consider the minimal polynomial $m_i \in \ints_K[t]$ of each $\alpha_i$. The relation $m_i(\alpha_i)=0$ gives the relation $m_i'(\alpha_i) d\alpha_i = 0$ by Proposition \ref{prop:differential_of_poly_is_derivative}. Such $m_i'(\alpha_i)$ is invertible in $\ints_L$ exactly when $\ints_K[\alpha_i]$ is an everywhere-unramified extension\footnote{This is because $m_i'(\alpha_i)$ generates the different $\mathfrak{D} := \mathfrak{D}_{K(\alpha_i)/K} \ideal \ints_{K}[\alpha_i]$ (cf. \cite[Prop. I.4.6(i)]{casselsFrohlich67}) which is divisible by a prime $\Prid$ of $\ints_K[\alpha_i]$ if and only if $\Prid$ ramifies. Therefore, $K(\alpha_i)/K$ is unramified if and only if $\mathfrak{D} :=(m_i'(\alpha_i))$ is not divisible by any prime ideals of $\ints_K[\alpha_i]$, i.e. if and only if $\mathfrak{D} = (1)$, i.e. if and only if $m_i'(\alpha_i)$ is invertible.} of $\ints_K$, so $d\alpha_i =0$ exactly when $\alpha_i$ generates an unramified extension. If $\{\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_s\}$ are the subset of generators which generate ramified extensions, then $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X} (D_f)$ is generated by $\{d\alpha_1, \ldots, d\alpha_s\}$. In particular, $L/K$ is everywhere-unramified if and only if $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X} = 0$;
		\item With exactly the same notation as (iii), we let $X' := \Spec(\ints_{K, S})$ and $Y' = \Spec(\ints_{L, S})$. Then since each $m'_i(\alpha_i)$ is invertible in $\ints_{K,S}$, we see that $\Omega^1_{Y' \mid X'} = 0$.
		% unramified extensions of ROIs of # flds
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}


\subsubsection{Finite \'etale morphisms, and their relations to smooth and unramified morphisms}
\label{subsubsec:2_4_3_sm_nr_et}
Recall the notion of the \bemph{residue field} $k(\prid)$ of a scheme $S$ at a point $\prid \in S$, which is defined the be the residue of the local ring $\ints_{S, \prid}$. 


\begin{defn}
	Let $f: X \to S$ be a morphism of schemes which is locally of finite type. We say that $f$ is \bemph{finite \'etale} (or \bemph{f\'et} for short) if the fibre $X_\prid := X \times_{S} \Spec(k(p))$ of $f$ at a point $\prid \in S$ is the spectrum of a finite \'etale $k(\prid)$-algebra (i.e. a finite sum of finite separable extensions of $k(p)$). In particular, a morphism $f : \Spec(B) \to \Spec(A)$ of affine schemes which is locally of finite type is finite \'etale if and only if $B \otimes_{A} k(\prid)$ is a finite \'etale $k(\prid)$-algebra for every $\prid \in S$. When a surjective morphism $f : X \epi S$ is finite \'etale, we may refer to it as a \bemph{finite \'etale cover}.
\end{defn}


\begin{eg}
	\label{eg:finite_etale_morphisms}
	\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
		\item Consider the morphism $\Gm' \epi \Gm;\, z \mapsto z^n$ from Example \ref{eg:kahler_differentials_schemes}(i). The residue field at $\prid \in \Gm$ is exactly the residue field of the local ring $\C[x,x^{-1}]_{\prid}$, i.e. it is given by $k(\prid) = \Frac(\C[x,x^{-1}]/\prid)$. The points of $\Gm = \Gm'$ are either closed or generic, since $\Gm$ is dimension 1 (this is just the Krull dimension for affine schemes), so at the generic point $\prid = (0) \in \Gm'$ we see that the fibre is the spectrum of $\C[x^{1/n}, x^{1/n}] \otimes_{\C[x,x^{-1}]} \C(x) = \C(x^{1/n})$, which is a separable extension of $\C(x)$. Closed points $\mxid \in \Gm$ are just maximal ideals of $\C[x,x^{-1}]$, which are all of the form $\mxid_a := (x-a, x^{-1} - a^{-1}) = (x-a)$ for some $a \in \C^\times$, and we see that 
			\[
				k(\mxid_a) = \frac{\C[x, x^{-1}]}{\mxid_a} = \frac{\C[x,x^{-1}]}{(x-a)} \cong \C.
			\]
		A closed point $\nxid_b := (t-b)$ of $\C[t,t^{-1}] \cong \C[x^{1/n}, x^{-1/n}]$ maps to $\mxid_a$ under this morphism if and only if $b^n = a$, so there are $n$ distinct points mapping to $\mxid_a$. So, we obtain that the fibre at $\mxid_a$ is the spectrum of 
		\[
			\C[t, t^{-1}] \otimes_{\C[x,x^{-1}]} \C \cong \frac{\C[t, t^{-1}]}{(t-\sqrt[n]{a})} \oplus \frac{\C[t, t^{-1}]}{(t-\zeta_n\sqrt[n]{a})} \oplus \cdots \oplus \frac{\C[t, t^{-1}]}{(t-\zeta_n^{n-1}\sqrt[n]{a})} \cong \C^{\oplus n},
		\]
		where $\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$. Since $\C/\C$ is trivially a finite separable extension, $\C^{\oplus n}$ is a finite \'etale $\C$-algebra and thus the morphism $\Gm' \epi \Gm;\, z \mapsto z^n$ is finite \'etale. We will see later that this is the equivalent of the $n$-fold cover of the circle $S^1 \epi S^1$ from the classical theory of covering spaces;
		\item If $L/K$ is a finite extension of number fields, unramified away from a finite set $S$ of primes of $\ints_K$. Then the map $\pi : \Spec(\ints_{L,S}) \epi \Spec(\ints_{K,S})$ induced by inclusion of $S$-integers $\pi^{\#}: \ints_{K,S} \mono \ints_{L,S}$ is finite \'etale. We'll see this in more detail in \S\ref{subsubsec:2_4_5_fet_maps_of_number_rings}.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

While this definition is nice and computable, we lose the benefit of geometric intuition. We will define some more geometric properties which will turn out to give an equivalent definition.

\begin{defn}
	Let $f: X \to S$ be a morphism of schemes which is locally of finite presentation. Then we say that $f$ is \bemph{unramified} if $\Omega^1_{X \mid S}=0$ with respect to $f$. We say that $f$ is \bemph{smooth at $x \in X$} there exists affine charts $U = \Spec(B) \ni x$ and $V = \Spec(A) \ni f(x)$, with $B = A[t_1, \ldots, t_n]/(p_1, \ldots, p_m)$, such that the $B$-ideal generated by the $m\times m$ minors of the matrix $(\partial p_i / \partial t_j)_{ij}$ is $B$. We say that $f$ is \bemph{smooth} if it is smooth at each $x \in X$.
\end{defn}

These are equivalent to the previous definitions when $f:X \to S$ is a morphism of $k$-schemes, but we omit the proof of this because it really isn't all too important for us.

\begin{thm}
	\label{thm:fet_iff_sm_and_unr}
	Let $f:X \to S$ be a morphism of schemes which is locally of finite presentation. Then $f$ is finite \'etale if and only if $f$ is both smooth and unramified.
\end{thm}
\begin{proof}
	Omitted; see \cite[Corollaire 17.6.2]{EGAIV}.
\end{proof}



\subsubsection{...for complex varieties}
\label{subsubsec:2_4_4_geometric_intuition}
% talk about Diego's ramification intuition in the morphisms-y bit
These definitions are all a bit abstract, so to give some intuition we'll see what this means for a morphism $f : Y \to X$ of affine complex varieties (such a map is automatically locally finitely presented). In this case, the closed points of each of these can be embedded into some $\C^n$ and $\C^m$ respectively, and thus can be endowed with the Euclidean topology to be made into complex manifolds. The corresponding complex manifolds will be denoted by $X^\text{an}$ and $Y^\text{an}$.\\

Since morphisms of varieties are polynomial maps, the map $f : Y \to X$ induces a map $f:Y^\text{an} \to X^\text{an}$ of manifolds. Let $q \in Y^\text{an}$ be a point and $p = f(q)$, both considered closed subvarieties of $Y$ and $X$ respectively which we may identify with $\Spec(\C)$. We will look at what both the `unramified' and `smooth' condition mean for this map $f: Y^\text{an} \to X^\text{an}$. 

\paragraph{Unramified:} Since dualising vector spaces $V \mapsto V^\vee$ is an involution, we get a series of identifications 
\[
	\Omega^1_{X \mid p} = \Omega^1_{\ints(X) \mid \C} = \Hom_{\C}(\Omega^1_{\ints(X) \mid \C}, \C)^\vee = \mathrm{Der}_\C(\ints(X), \C)^\vee = T_p^* X^\text{an}
\] 
where $T_p^*X^\text{an}$ denotes the cotangent space of $X^\text{an}$ at $p$, and similarly $\Omega^1_{Y\mid q} = T_q^* Y^\text{an}$. Then the canonical exact sequence induced by the map\footnote{\Exc. Look back at Example \ref{eg:kahler_differentials_rings}(i) to see if you can figure out what the map is yourself :)} $\Omega^1_{X \mid p} \to \Omega^1_{Y \mid p}$ is of the form:
\[
	\underbrace{\Omega^1_{X \mid p}}_{T_p^*X^\text{an}} \longrightarrow \underbrace{\Omega^1_{Y \mid q}}_{T_q^*Y^\text{an}} \longrightarrow \Omega^1_{Y \mid X} \longrightarrow 0,
\]
for which the first arrow may be identified with the adjoint $(d_q f)^\vee$ of the derivative $d_q f : T_q Y^\text{an} \to T_p X^\text{an}$ of $f$ at $q$. So we see that $f$ is unramified, i.e. $\Omega^1_{Y \mid X} = 0$, if and only if $(d_qf)^\vee$ is surjective. That is to say, $f$ is unramified if and only if the derivative $d_q f$ is \emph{injective}. The point here is that when unramified morphisms don't fold away dimensions.

\paragraph{Smooth:} This one does not just translate to `$f$ is a smooth map', but rather that $f$ is a smooth \emph{submersion} (by the submersion theorem). That is, $f$ is algebraically smooth exactly when the derivative $d_q f : T_q Y^\text{an} \to T_p X^\text{an}$ is \emph{surjective}.\\


Together, these give us a nice characterisation of finite \'etale morphisms of affine varieties over $\C$:
\begin{thm}
	\label{thm:fet_maps_complex_varieties}
	A morphism $f: Y \to X$ of affine complex varieties is finite \'etale if and only if the corresponding map $f:Y^\text{an} \to X^\text{an}$ of complex manifolds is a local diffeomorphism.
\end{thm}

This should give us some nice intuition: we should think of a general finite \'etale morphism as the algebraic equivalent of a local diffeomorphism!


\subsubsection{...and for number rings}
\label{subsubsec:2_4_5_fet_maps_of_number_rings}
Another example of interest of us is extensions of number fields, and their assoicated $S$-integers. Once again, in this example the `finite \'etale' condition is easy to characterise, although this time it is in terms of arithmetic data rather than geometric data.\\ 

Let $L/K$ be a finite extension of number fields, and let $S$ be a set of prime ideals of $\ints_K$. We recall that the ring $\ints_{K,S}$ of $S$-integers is formed from $\ints_K$ by inverting generators of all of the prime ideals $\prid \in S$. When $\hat{S}$ is the set of primes of $\ints_L$ above all of those in $S$, we may write $\ints_{L,S} := \ints_{L, \hat{S}}$ for the $\hat{S}$-integers of $L$. The inclusion $K \mono L$ then restricts to an inclusion $\pi^{\#} : \ints_{K, S} \mono \ints_{L, S}$ of $S$-integers, which in turn induces a morphism $\pi : \Spec(\ints_{L, S}) \epi \Spec(\ints_{K,S})$ of affine schemes. Such a morphism is always locally finitely presented.\\

At the generic point $(0) \in \Spec(\ints_{K,S})$, the residue field of $\Spec(\ints_{K,S})$ is given by $\Frac(\ints_{K,S}) = K$, and so the fibre of $\pi$ at $(0)$ is given by the spectrum of
\[
	\ints_{L,S} \otimes_{\ints_{K,S}} K = L,
\]
which is always a finite \'etale $K$-algebra, since $L/K$ is a separable extension. But at a closed point $\prid \in \Spec(\ints_{K,S})$, things get more interesting. A nonzero prime ideal of $\ints_{K,S}$ may be identified with a prime ideal of $\ints_K$ not contained in $S$, and so we take this convention. The fibre of $\pi$ here is given by the spectrum of
\[
	\ints_{L,S} \otimes_{\ints_{K,S}} \left(\frac{\ints_{K,S}}{\prid}\right) = \frac{\ints_{L,S}}{\prid\ints_{L,S}} = \frac{\ints_L}{\prid\ints_L}.
\]
Let $\Prid_1,\ldots, \Prid_r$ be the primes of $\ints_L$ above $\prid$ (each pairwise distinct) and $e_1, \ldots, e_r \in \N$ such that $\prid\ints_L = \Prid_1^{e_1} \cdots \Prid_r^{e_r}$, then Sunzi's theorem tells us that 
\[
	\tag{$\ast$}
	\frac{\ints_L}{\prid\ints_L} = \frac{\ints_L}{\Prid_1^{e_1}\cdots\Prid_r^{e_r}} \cong \frac{\ints_L}{\Prid_1^{e_1}} \oplus \cdots \oplus \frac{\ints_L}{\Prid_r^{e_r}}.
\]
In particular, when each $e_i=1$ we see that this is a finite sum of finite separable extensions of $k(\prid) = \ints_K/\prid$. If $e_{i_0}>1$ for some $i_0$, we see that $\ints_L/\Prid_{i_0}^{e_{i_0}}$ has nilpotents, so $\ints_L/\Prid_{i_0}^{e_{i_0}}$ cannot be a finite \'etale over $k(\prid)$. Consequently, neither can the RHS of ($\ast$) be a finite \'etale algebra over $k(\prid)$. So $\ints_L/\prid\ints_L$ is finite \'etale over $k(\prid)$ if and only if all of the $e_i=1$, that is, if and only if $\prid$ is unramified in $L/K$. Thus...

\begin{thm}
	\label{thm:fet_covers_of_number_rings}
	Let $L/K$ be a finite extension of number fields, and $S$ a finite set of primes of $\ints_K$. The morphism $\pi_S : \Spec(\ints_{L,S}) \epi \Spec(\ints_{K,S})$ induced by inclusion of $S$-integers is finite \'etale if and only if $S$ contains all of the primes ramified in $L/K$. In particular, $\pi_\varnothing : \Spec(\ints_L) \epi \Spec(\ints_K)$ is finite \'etale if and only if $L/K$ is everywhere unramified.
\end{thm}
